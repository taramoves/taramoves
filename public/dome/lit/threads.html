Three threads the researcher named as present in the braid but not yet addressed here, each with the evidence that already exists in the 34-source corpus, and then the adjacent disciplines that could carry them further. This page is a worklist, not an argument: confidence: medium because most of what follows is a direction, not a finding.

1. Archives of digital media

The corpus contains no study of archiving a dome performance, but it contains the pieces of one, and they disagree about what an archive is for.

  • The dome is already described as an object that must be re-mediated to survive. Bay-Cheng catches a theatre doing it by accident: "That Oligny's final selection was shown in a loop on an improperly used, closed-circuit TV, without aerial or cable connection and turned into an outdated slide projector, can be considered remediation with a vengeance" — Bay-Cheng, Mapping Intermediality in Performance, PDF p.216
  • The database is the other candidate form for a performance's afterlife — Manovich's chapter V runs The Database Logic (PDF p.194), Database and Narrative (PDF p.199), A Database Complex (PDF p.205), Database Cinema: Greenaway and Vertov (PDF p.207), and then Navigable space (PDF p.213). The pairing matters: the archive of a performance is either a narrative or a database, and he treats them as opposed.
  • Grau supplies the historical correction, and it is quotable: "new media do not render old ones obsolete, but rather assign them new places within the system" — Grau, Virtual Art, PDF p.23 — so an archive is not a graveyard but a re-assignment of what a previous medium is allowed to be.
  • Dixon, even in preview form, shows where performance studies takes this: his chapter 22 is on memory, and it works through "Remediations of the visionary 'Memory Palace' (aka 'Memory Theatre') project of Italian Guilio Camillo (1480–1544) by artists such as Emil Hrvatin and Stephen Wilson" — Dixon, Digital Performance, PDF p.38. A performance in a dome that is about memory is working in a lineage that runs back to a theatre built as a memory device, and the corpus already knows this.

  • The researcher's own practice is inside this thread: Barrett & Bolt's exegesis is a claim that practice produces knowledge that then has to be written down, i.e. an archive built by the practitioner. See knowledge-made-by-making.

  • The closest thing in the corpus to a document that records a live event is in the Schlemmer material, and it records how little survives: "The only documentation of the performance consists of a few black and white photographs, as well as a partially completed information form about the dance, and a drawing for the stage set-up." — Feuerstein, Oskar Schlemmer's Vordruck: The Making of an Architectural Body for the Bauhaus, PDF p.115 / printed p.103. She also puts the distance between a plan and an event into one sentence: "A drawing of the plan may have been used as a guide rather than as the template for the dance." — Feuerstein, Oskar Schlemmer's Vordruck, PDF p.119 / printed p.106.

  • A caution this thread has to carry, because the obvious reading is wrong: the Vordruck is not a notation of a performance. It is a printed proportion sheet — "On the page distributed to the students appears a drawing o f a male figure shown from the front, side, and back, and the word Vordruck." (PDF p.47 / printed p.34) — made for Schlemmer's Bauhaus course Der Mensch, drawn over to mark anatomy, and reused as a flat pattern for costumes. The notation material proper (Laban's system, and Schlemmer's own graphic record of the 1927/28 dances) is a single paragraph in the dissertation, not its argument (PDF p.40 / printed pp.27–28). An archive thread should cite this dissertation for the document made to be drawn on, not for a score-and-event theory it does not contain.

What is missing: nothing in these 45 sources discusses the archiving of a live dome event — no discussion of vaulting a show, of dome masters as records, of what happens to a performance that only exists as a set of files, or of the ethics of who holds a community's dome content.

2. The rectangle inside the dome (skeuomorphism)

The strongest single find in this thread is that the field's own authoring standard keeps the square at the centre of the hemisphere:

  • "Without the dome masters (a circular grid that defines a hemisphere within a square), one could not easily produce media for fulldome modalities." — Saper & Tomlinson, PDF p.9 — the format is a circle inscribed in a rectangle, and every dome show passes through that rectangle to exist.

Three more pieces of evidence, all from the corpus:

  • The dome's software does not abolish the flat image, it re-projects it: "The virtual dome was still composed of flat two-dimensional images." — Moser & MacLeod, Immersed in Technology, PDF p.315 — in the Placeholder studies, a photographed landscape was hand-shaped onto terrain, a literal skeuomorphic transfer from a slide to a mound.
  • Manovich describes exactly the mechanism by which a medium's interface becomes its invisible ontology: "far from being a transparent window into the data inside a computer, the interface bring with it strong messages of its own" — Manovich, The Language of New Media, PDF p.76 / printed p.57. A rectangle that survives in a dome survives because the tools are rectangular, not because anyone decided it should.
  • The seating finishes the job: "Most seating arrangements in planetariums and dome theaters have a regretful tendency to reintroduce the limitation of the field of vision that the 360-degree projection technology has overcome." — Remann & Francis, PDF p.25.

The thread has a history, and it is older than fulldome by half a century. The Bauhaus volume now in the corpus contains a dome — Andreas Weininger's Kugeltheater, a plate and signed text, cross-referenced by Schlemmer (PDF p.89):

  • "A sphere as architectonic structure in place of the customary theater. The spectators, on the inner wall of the sphere, find themselves in a new relationship to space. Because of their all-encompassing view, because of centripetal force, they find themselves in a new psychic, optical, acoustical relationship; they find themselves confronted with new possibilities for concentric, eccentric, multidirectional, mechanical space-stage phenomena." — Weininger, signed A. W., in Gropius (ed.), The Theater of the Bauhaus, PDF p.90 / printed p.89.
  • The same volume names what the sphere was proposed to replace, and the word it reaches for is not rectangle: "It is clear that the present peep-show stage is not suitable for such organized motion." — Moholy-Nagy, PDF p.69 / printed p.68. Schlemmer puts the rectangle's conditions (wall behind, wings at the sides, one frontal audience) at the origin of theatre rather than in its conventions (PDF p.96 / printed p.95); Gropius wants the "flat picture effect" overcome (PDF p.15 / printed p.14); Molnár breaks the frame by specification at 270 degrees (PDF p.74 / printed p.73); and Moholy writes projection into the performance space as a medium of its own (PDF p.68 / printed p.67) — the earliest point in the corpus where projection is a stage element rather than a backdrop.
  • The limit travels with the claim: nothing in this volume was performed in a sphere, and Weininger's theater is published as a project. The dome enters theatre history as a proposal, not a precedent — which is exactly the position the researcher's own work is in.

So the thread is not "the dome escapes the frame" — that is frame's existing argument — it is "the rectangle is re-installed at three levels: the authoring file, the production pipeline, and the furniture" — with a hundred-year-old critique of the peep-show stage behind it that the fulldome literature does not cite. The slide projector in this story is not a predecessor that was superseded; it is the shape the pipeline still has. See hidden-apparatus.

What is missing: no source in the corpus treats the slide projector as a format that shaped fulldome, and none historicises the dome master — the Bauhaus volume supplies the pre-history the field lacks, and the field does not know it is there.

3. Memory outside the body, and what it does to orientation

The braid's largest cluster and the corpus's narrowest one: memory is almost absent here — Hayles, de Certeau and Sloterdijk are the only sources that touch it.

  • The externalised-memory claim has a media-theoretical form in Manovich's navigable space (PDF p.213) and The Poetics of Navigation (PDF p.223): when a world is stored as a database, moving through it becomes the cultural form, and the navigator is a distinct figure from the explorer (PDF p.231, The Navigator and the Explorer).
  • De Certeau gives the older, bodily version of the same problem: "It thus seems possible to give a preliminary definition of walking as a space of enunciation." — de Certeau, The Practice of Everyday Life, PDF p.123 / printed p.98 — knowledge of a place used to be carried in the body's routes; see body-as-instrument.
  • Sloterdijk supplies what the substitution costs: enclosure has become prosthetic, "But now that God's shimmering bubbles, the celestial domes, have burst, who could have the power to create prosthetic husks around those who have been exposed?" — Sloterdijk, PDF p.12 / printed p.24, discussed at length in frame — a shell that remembers for us.
  • The question the researcher actually asks in the work — which way is north — becomes, under this thread, a question about whether orientation is a competence of the body or a service provided by a device. Lynch is the corpus's one source that treats orientation as a designed capacity, and the sentence he gives for it holds sensation and memory together: "In the process of way-finding, the strategic link is the environmental image, the generalized mental picture of the exterior physical world that is held by an individual. This image is the product both of immediate sensation and of the memory of past experience, and it is used to interpret information and to guide action." — Lynch, The Image of the City, PDF p.8. His imageability is the design term for it — "that quality in a physical object which gives it a high probability of evoking a strong image in any given observer" (PDF p.10) — and the image itself is co-produced rather than received: "Environmental images are the result of a two-way process between the observer and his environment." (PDF p.9 / printed p.6). He is not writing about audiences; he is writing about inhabitants, which is a different relation to a space.

  • Turner is the corpus's account of what a gathered body in a bounded place becomes, and his terms come with a cost that has to be stated in the same breath: liminality and communitas describe obligatory ritual among the Ndembu, not voluntary spectatorship at an event. Citing him for a dome audience imports a communal obligation the audience never agreed to. See the correction below on what is not in his book.

  • Bligh supplies the corpus's one empirical account of what a seated audience takes from unbroken exposition, and he also quotes the best description of the lecture as a form performed in time — then immediately doubts it: "McLeish (1976) claims the lecture 'is undoubtedly the most economical method by which the individual can present in a personalized and continuous argument the general framework for understanding the fundamentals of a particular subject, and involving the audience in reflective thought that moves in time with the on-going performance'. But I do doubt it." — Bligh, What's the Use of Lectures?, PDF p.2 / printed pp.8–9. A performance-lecture inherits both halves: the claim that a performed argument moves in time with its audience's thought, and the doubt about whether it produces any. His verdicts on the four objective types (information: as good as other methods, no better; thought, attitudes, behavioural skills: worse than discussion) are the baseline any claim about a dome lecture has to beat or accept.

The learning consequence, which is the part that bears on the lecture form: if memory is externalised, then a lecture's job shifts from storage to navigation and orientation — and the evidence that audiences are poor judges of their own learning arrives separately, from outside this corpus (see the leads list below). That is a claim the review will have to build from sources it does not yet hold.

Corrections the new annotations forced

Recorded here because each one is a claim that would otherwise travel into the review wrongly.

  • "Liminoid" is not in The Ritual Process. Zero hits in the file; the term belongs to Turner's later work (From Ritual to Theatre, 1982). Anyone citing this volume for the liminal/liminoid distinction is citing a book the distinction is not in.
  • "A realm of pure possibility" is not from this book either. It belongs to The Forest of Symbols (1967) — and the Cheng annotation already filed in this corpus attributes it as if it sat next to The Ritual Process. Check the source before the phrase travels further.
  • The Vordruck is not a notation — see thread 1. The dissertation is about the body in architecture, and its archival argument is scattered rather than argued.
  • Two of the eleven new files are book reviews, not the books they are named after. White's Audience Participation in Theatre is present only as Grant Tyler Peterson's review (Participations 11(1), 2014 — the journal name is not printed in the file); Kendon's Conducting Interaction only as Christian Heath's review (American Anthropologist 94, 1992). Neither book's argument can be cited from this folder.
  • The Lofland file is about 5% of the book — contents, preface, prologue and chapter 1 up to p.12, stopping mid-sentence. Her public-realm definition and the private/parochial/public trichotomy are quotable from it; the erosion thesis and the weak-ties argument are not in the file at all, and "spectator", "theatre" and "performance" have zero hits in it.
  • Lynch's text layer is damaged in exactly the passage this thread needs. The legibility definition prints as "It_will", "parrs", "pattern/Just", "legible.city" (PDF p.7), and imageability prints spaced as "image ability". Use a print copy before quoting those verbatim; the way-finding quote above (PDF p.8) came out clean.
  • The Gropius volume's "utopia" material is Schlemmer's, not Molnár's. Molnár's U-Theater is a numbered building specification; the Schlemmer text is the one already annotated separately in frame.
