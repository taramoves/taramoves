The rest of this wiki annotates sources — arguments about the form. This page holds the works, so the review can describe the genre from the inside rather than from its critics.

Read this first. Eight works were named. Two are already in the corpus and read (Schlemmer's essay, and his demonstration-lecture practice via the Bauhaus volume and the Vordruck dissertation). The other six are not on disk and not watched — every statement about them below comes from the sources named in the entry (publisher, collection page, interview, review), and nothing is quoted from a work that has not been read. The Cage quotations are the exception: they come from a scan of Silence that was read line by line for this page, with the page markers recorded. Treat this page as a syllabus with honest provenance, not as an annotated bibliography.

The spectrum, arranged

The researcher's own axis runs theatrical → performance lecture → actual lecture. Placing the eight on it, most-lecture first:

Reading it that way, the axis is not "less lecture / more theatre". It is how much of the lecture's apparatus stays on stage: at 1 the apparatus is the work, at 7 the apparatus has been replaced by a story that argues.

1. John Cage, Lecture on Nothing

Written around 1949; Cage's own account, in the Foreword to Silence, is the delivery record: "So it was that I gave about 1949 my Lecture on Nothing at the Artists' Club on Eighth Street in New York City (the artists' club started by Robert Motherwell, which predated the popular one associated with Philip Pavia, Bill de Kooning, et al.). This Lecture on Nothing was written in the same rhythmic structure I employed at the time in my musical compositions (Sonatas and Interludes, Three Dances, etc.)." — Foreword (p. viii of the scan). He also records that "One of the structural divisions was the repetition, some fourteen times, of a single page in which occurred the refrain, 'If anyone is sleepy let him go to sleep.'", that Jeanne Reynal "stood up part way through, screamed, and then said, while I continued speaking, 'John, I dearly love you, but I can't bear another minute.' She then walked out", and that "Later, during the question period, I gave one of six previously prepared answers regardless of the question asked. This was a reflection of my engagement in Zen." — same page. Asked by M. C. Richards why he did not simply give "a conventional informative lecture", he answered: "I don't give these lectures to surprise people, but out of a need for poetry." — Foreword (p. ix).

The performance instruction printed at the head of the piece is the most useful thing here for a practice-based researcher, because it is a delivery rule embedded in the text: "There are four measures in each line and twelve lines in each unit of the rhythmic structure. There are forty-eight such units, each having forty-eight measures. The whole is divided into five large parts, in the proportion 7, 6, 14, 14, 7. […] The text is printed in four columns to facilitate a rhythmic reading. Each line is to be read across the page from left to right, not down the columns in sequence." — Lecture on Nothing, p. 109.

Provenance and limits. Quoted from the Internet Archive scan of Cage, Silence: Lectures and Writings (Wesleyan University Press, 1961), read for this page; page numbers are taken from the running heads in the scan ("FOREWORD/ix", "108/SILENCE") and the OCR damage is retained where it occurs ("he" for "be", "hut" for "but"). The famous refrain "I have nothing to say / and I am saying it" is present in the scan but its line order is not verifiable from it — the piece is set in four columns and the djvu text of that page reads across them, so the words can be confirmed and their sequence cannot. Quote it only from a printed copy.

What it does for the argument. The score/event problem the lecture-as-form page already carries, in its purest form: a lecture whose content is its structure, with a duration and a page count as compositional parameters. It is also the corpus's missing art-historical lineage arriving at last — Schlemmer's demonstration-lectures (below) and Cage's lecture as composition are the two ends of the same century.

2. David Byrne, Envisioning Emotional Epistemological Information (2003)

A boxed set: 96-page book plus a multi-format DVD containing five PowerPoint presentations with original music, published by Steidl in co-production with Pace/MacGill Gallery, New York (out of print). The publisher's description is explicit about the operation: it is "about taking subjective, even emotional, information and presenting it in a familiar audiovisual form — using a medium in a way that is different, and possibly better, than what was intended", and about "appropriating a contemporary, corporate staple and making something critical, beautiful and humorous with it" (Steidl). The New York Times review (Veronique Vienne, 17 Aug 2003) describes "arrows that curve out of their trajectory and into psychedelic rainbow-colored curlicues, surreal charts that satirize postmodern posturing, typographical compositions that present absurd abstractions with straight-faced conviction".

Why it belongs on this page rather than in a design survey. This is threads-to-develop thread 2 with an artist attached: the rectangle as an emotional and rhetorical instrument. The dome's content pipeline is rectangular because its authoring tools are (see the dome-master "hemisphere within a square", Saper & Tomlinson PDF p.9); Byrne takes the same rectangle and pushes in the opposite direction — not filling it with sky, but exposing what the format does to whatever is put inside it. A dome piece that quotes the slide format as costume is making an argument the corpus currently cannot cite from anywhere except Byrne and Manovich.

Limit. Nothing here is quoted from the book or DVD; the statements above are the publisher's and the reviewer's. The work itself has not been watched.

3. Will Stephen, How to Sound Smart in Your TEDx Talk (2015)

A five-minute talk given at TEDxNewYork in January 2015, capping a day of talks: the performer delivers a talk that has no content and demonstrates, in real time, the gestures that make a talk sound substantive — "talk with your hands and use expressive inflection to project confidence; ask the audience questions; share a personal anecdote". The official blurb is the joke stated plainly: "foolproof presentation skills to make you sound brilliant — even if you are literally saying nothing."

What it does for the argument. It is the domestic version of the corpus's learning problem. Bligh's extract in this corpus gives the measured baseline (a lecture transmits information as well as other methods and no better; it is weaker than discussion for thought, attitudes and skills — Expanded_Bligh, PDF pp.1–9), and the study the review still needs — Deslauriers et al., "Measuring Actual Learning versus Feeling of Learning" — is the one that separates the feeling of having learned from the measurement. Stephen performs that gap for five minutes: the audience's sense of substance is produced entirely by delivery. For anyone writing a dome lecture, this is the failure mode with the sound turned up: an audience can be made to feel taught.

4. Look Around You (BBC Two, 2002; second series 2005)

Eight "modules" in the first series, devised and written by Robert Popper and Peter Serafinowicz, parodying British school and science television of the 1970s and 80s — the Open-University-style talking head, the diagram, the authoritative voice-over, the deadpan caption, the experiment that explains nothing. The second series moves to a 1980s studio format.

Why it matters to a dome practice. The planetarium show inherits exactly this genre — a seated audience, a narrated didactic sequence, images illustrating a voice — and the corpus's fulldome literature mostly discusses it without naming it. Look Around You is the corpus's missing critical vocabulary for the didactic register, performed as form: the parody only works because the format is that legible. It is a companion to the planetarium items in the fulldome section, and to lecture-as-form's account of narration and the talking head.

5. Oskar Schlemmer: "Man and Art Figure" (1925) and the Dessau stage demonstrations

The historical anchor, and the one item on this list the corpus holds in full. Two things are in the folder: the essay "Man and Art Figure" in Die Bühne im Bauhaus (the Bauhausbücher 4 volume Schlemmer co-authored with Gropius and Moholy-Nagy), and the demonstration-lecture practice itself.

The decisive sentence is Schlemmer's own designation of work that cannot be staged: "Or he may isolate himself altogether from the existing theater and cast his anchor far out into the sea of fantasy and distant possibilities. In this case his projects remain paper and model, materials for demonstration lectures" — Schlemmer, Man and Art Figure, PDF p.18 / printed p.31. The unrealisable project is not a failure; it is reclassified as lecture material, by the artist, in 1925. That is the performance-lecture lineage stated at its source.

What the demonstration actually was is documented in the dissertation in the corpus, which reads the format as a genre with its own history: "The staged lecture-demonstration format might be compared to and understood in relation to an ideal space inhabited by an ideal body, translating Vitruvius's description of the first building (iheAedium) as stage and the perfectly proportioned body (III. 1) as performer." — Feuerstein, PDF p.81 / printed pp.68–69; and the same page notes that "Schlemmer's workshop and performances happened on stage, rather than in a theater." The Bauhaus volume also contains the period's largest surviving demonstration-lecture script — Schlemmer's Theater (Bühne), the 1927 lecture-demonstration, which is the longest text in that volume (PDF pp.80–103).

The borrow, with its cost. Schlemmer is the right ancestor and a dangerous one: his demonstrations happen in a workshop, to students, about a body being measured — the audience is being trained, not entertained, and there is no dome, no projection and no screen in his account (his projected flats are scenery used as screens; see frame). Cite him for the lineage of the demonstration-lecture, not for an audience theory.

6. Andrea Fraser, Official Welcome (2001)

A 30-minute video of a live performance, written, directed and performed by Fraser, commissioned by the MICA Foundation and first performed in November 2001 for invited guests at the New York home of the Foundation's founders — the piece appropriates the "official welcome" that introduced MICA's commissions. In the artist's own account of the work (Generali Foundation collection record), she "begins with the artist thanking her patron-hosts and acknowledging the work of the Foundation. Imperceptibly, however, the artist's position changes from speaking about her patrons — [to] about herself. She continues to shift roles, working her way through nine different pairs of artists and their supporters with a script based on fragments and artist's statements and interviews, patron profiles, critics' essays and curators['] honorific speeches." The work is "situationally specific": the body of the script is fixed, but the introduction is rewritten for each host, who is asked to write about the artist — "This material is then incorporated into a new introduction." A 2003 version at Kunstverein Hamburg (its introduction drawn from a statement by Yilmaz Dziewior) was documented on video and projected near the exhibition entrance for the show's duration. Mid-performance she strips, saying "I'm not a person today. I'm an object in an art work" (as quoted in Monoskop's account of the Hamburg version).

What it does for the argument. This is the lecture as position, which is the gap the corpus names at the-gap: what the person at the front is, institutionally, while they speak. Every device Fraser uses is available to a practice-based researcher — the composite script assembled from other people's words, the speaker's position as the subject, the introduction that changes with the room. Its method is the same as an exegesis (see knowledge-made-by-making) reversed: instead of writing about practice, the practice is the writing about itself, spoken.

Limit. Not watched for this page; the description is the artist's own published account plus a secondary summary, and the one quoted line is a quotation of a quotation.

7. Hannah Gadsby, Nanette (2018)

Recorded at the Sydney Opera House and released on Netflix in 2018, after touring from 2017; it starts as conventional stand-up, then stops being one — Gadsby argues with her own form on stage, announces mid-show that she is quitting comedy, and delivers an argument about self-deprecation, homophobia and men's violence with the jokes removed. In her own words in a later interview (NPR, June 2019): "Well, it was always a theatrical conceit … So I quit in the middle of the show, and then I stopped doing comedy." Her own account of the mechanics is the useful part: the show works by deconstructing a joke after telling it, and by taking a last-minute joke about being beaten up and re-telling it as the true story it was (TV Guide interview: "the show was originally a response to Australia's gay marriage debate"). Her 2019 talk Three ideas. Three contradictions. Or not. (a TED talk about the same material) is worth having alongside it: it is a lecture about the performance, in which she explains the rule of three and the broken contract.

What it does for the argument. Nanette is the strongest available demonstration that a performed argument can carry a thesis and be judged as one — and it is the case the corpus's own contradiction predicts: the-corpus-argues-with-itself records Muñoz on the transformative promise of performance against Fisher on its institutional limits; here the performer says outright that the form had to be broken before it could hold the content, and the audience's laughter is the thing being interrogated. For a dome lecture, the transferable question is the one lecture-as-form asks: when the argument stops being comfortable, does the apparatus help or hide?

Limit. Described from reviews and interviews, including the performer's own; not analysed from a recording for this page.

What this list is missing, and the obvious next additions

  • No dome example, and no planetarium performance-lecture. The closest the corpus gets is Manovich's St Petersburg planetarium performance (PDF p.32) and the SAT/IX Symposium dome piece with the seats cleared (Remann & Francis, PDF p.19). If a dome lecture-performance exists on record, the review needs it and it is not here.
  • The science-demonstration lineage: the Royal Institution Christmas Lectures (Faraday's from 1827), the Victorian public lecture-demonstration, the museum talk.
  • Robert Wilson's staging of Lecture on Nothing — reported, unverified; worth chasing because it makes Cage's text a performed object again.
  • The contemporary art lecture-performance: Walid Raad's Atlas Group lectures, Rabih Mroué, Hito Steyerl's lecture films, Mark Leckey's performances — see the leads table in threads-to-develop.
  • The monologue-as-lecture: Spalding Gray's Swimming to Cambodia, Laurie Anderson's Nerve Bible — the performed first-person account that argues.
