Abstract

Seated audience members interact with a dome through a web app on their phones. The project has a technological part and a narrative part; this document covers only the technology. Tabs describe the parts of the system — calibration, interactions, the facilitator’s scenes, and the hub — and a last one sets out the build plan.

    1Overview

    1.1Three parts

    The phone runs a web app. The hub is a server at the venue that receives everything phones send, decides what it means, logs it, and drives the audiovisual output. The dome is the projection and spatial audio.

    1.3Phones are for participating

    People use their phone during the experience to take part. For the web app this means:

    • Big and bold buttons. The press area is large, so it doesn’t take a nimble touch to press button(s).
    • Dark by default. Screens are black between interactions and dim during them, so phones don't light up the room or wash out projection.
    • Screen stays on. The app keeps the screen awake so the phone never locks mid-scene.
    • Leaving is logged. The browser reports when the page is hidden; the hub logs it (Section 5.3).
    • At the door. Ask people to turn on Do Not Disturb. A web page cannot block notifications itself.

    2Calibrate

    The phone takes a picture of a marker on the dome, and from that the hub works out which way the phone faces and where its owner sits. Until then it has no cursor.

    2.1What the phone knows on its own

    The back of the phone is the pointer, like aiming a camera; the screen faces the person. The browser reports three angles \(( \alpha, \beta, \gamma )\).

    • Tilt is reliable. It is measured against gravity.
    • Heading is not. The browser measures \(\alpha\) from an arbitrary starting direction: on iPhones, wherever the phone faced when the page opened; on other phones, a compass that steel and electronics in the building pull off. Every phone is off by its own unknown angle.
    • Seat is unknown. The phone has no idea where in the room it is.

    2.2Why the seat matters

    People sit all over the dome. Someone off-centre who aims at a spot points in a different direction than someone at the centre would. At distance \(d\) from the centre of a dome of radius \(R\), the difference is up to

    \[ \varepsilon_{\max} = \arcsin\frac{d}{R}. \]

    Drag the seat P or the target T. The filled dot is where the person aims. The hollow dot is where the cursor would land if the hub ignored the seat and treated everyone as sitting at the centre.

    2.3The calibration picture

    The dome shows a marker: five bright dots in an X at the top of the dome, with one tip drawn as a ring so the phone can tell which way is front. The person looks up and points the back of the phone at it; the phone takes the picture.

    The hub knows each dot's real position on the dome. A dot at azimuth \(\theta\) (clockwise from the front) and elevation \(\phi\) (0° at the horizon, 90° at the top) on a dome of radius \(R\) is at

    \[ \mathbf{X} = R\,(\cos\phi\sin\theta,\ \cos\phi\cos\theta,\ \sin\phi). \]

    The camera sees each dot at a pixel \((u, v)\). A camera at position \(\mathbf{t}\) with orientation \(Q\) and focal length \(f\) sees a point \(\mathbf{X}\) where

    \[ s\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\\1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}f&0&c_u\\0&f&c_v\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix} Q^{\top}\,(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{t}). \]

    Five dots give ten equations. Solving them (the perspective-n-point problem) gives the phone's seat position \(\mathbf{t}\) and its true orientation \(Q\). Comparing \(Q\) with the phone's own angles at the same moment gives its heading offset \(\delta\).

    • A short burst, not one frame. Browsers don't report focal length. The phone takes about 20 frames while the hand moves slightly; the sensors say how much the phone turned, the images show how far the dots moved, and that gives the focal length. It also works whichever rear lens the phone picks.
    • Curve of the dome. Included exactly, because the dots' 3D positions are used, not a flat picture.
    • Privacy. The phone finds the dots itself and sends only their pixel positions. No images leave the phone.
    • Check. Afterwards the person aims at a mark at the front and taps. If the cursor isn't on it, calibrate again.

    2.4Pointing, once calibrated

    The phone streams its angles about 30 times a second. With the rotation \(R = R_z(\alpha')\,R_x(\beta)\,R_y(\gamma)\), where \(\alpha' = \alpha + \delta\), the back of the phone points along

    \[ \mathbf{f} = R\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\-1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}-\cos\alpha'\sin\gamma-\sin\alpha'\sin\beta\cos\gamma\\ -\sin\alpha'\sin\gamma+\cos\alpha'\sin\beta\cos\gamma\\ -\cos\beta\cos\gamma\end{bmatrix}. \]

    The pointing line starts at the seat \(\mathbf{t}\), not the centre, and meets the dome at

    \[ \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{t} + s\,\mathbf{f}, \qquad s = -\,\mathbf{t}\cdot\mathbf{f} + \sqrt{(\mathbf{t}\cdot\mathbf{f})^2 - \lVert\mathbf{t}\rVert^2 + R^2}, \]

    which is the cursor, in dome angles \(\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(X_x, X_y)\) and \(\phi = \arcsin(X_z / R)\). The hub smooths it, since hands shake, and shows it on the dome as that person's mark (Section 3).

    2.5Where people are relative to each other

    Each phone's position \(\mathbf{t}\) is in dome coordinates, so the hub ends up with a map of the audience.

    • Accuracy. Roughly 0.3 m, enough to tell rows and neighbours apart.
    • Seat plan. If the venue's seat positions are known, each estimate can snap to the nearest seat.
    • Hand, not head. It measures where the phone is, usually 30–50 cm in front of the person.
    • Uses. Correct pointing from every seat, sound from your part of the room, teams by area, interactions between neighbours.

    2.6What the person does

    1. Tap Start. Motion and camera access are requested.
    2. Point camera at the marker. The phone takes the picture on its own.
    3. Aim at the front mark and tap to confirm.

    About ten seconds. The marker can be shown again at any point for latecomers or to correct drift.

    3Interactions

    Everything a person can do is a short chain: get something, act, and the dome answers.

    An object is only what a person is holding at that moment. Once it is placed, dropped or sent it stops being an object and becomes part of what the dome is showing, along with everything else there: marks, unheld words, tallies, graphics, things still in flight. Grab is the way back — the hub hands a piece of that content to whoever presses on it, if nobody else is holding it.

    3.1The mark

    Whenever a phone is aiming, the dome shows its mark at the cursor (Section 2.4) — the object if the person is holding one, a plain cursor if not — so people always know where they are. Everything below happens on top of that mark.

    3.2Getting an object

    Getting is itself a phone action; it just happens on the phone screen rather than on the dome, and its result is that the person's mark becomes the object. Grabbing is the exception: the object is already on the dome and is claimed by pressing on it.

    Table 2: Where objects come from.
    SourcePhone screenWhat is sent
    Assigned“You have the triangle”Nothing; the hub assigns it
    ChooseGrid of optionsThe choice
    DrawDrawing padStrokes, saved by the hub as an image file
    WriteText fieldThe text
    PhotoCameraA resized image, uploaded as a file
    GrabNone; press on it on the domeNothing; the hub hands it over if it is free

    Drawings and photos are uploaded to the hub as files; only the file name travels on to the dome. Photos go through an approval queue before they appear, since a public projection needs someone to check what's on it. The camera is already allowed from calibration.

    3.3Actions and results

    The phone only ever does five things. What the dome makes of each is set by the scene, not by the phone.

    Table 3: Phone actions and the results they can produce.
    Phone actionUses the cursorDome results
    Get—Your mark becomes the object
    Aim onlyyesCounted by region
    TapyesLeaves a mark; leaves the object; erases at that spot
    Hold, move, releaseyesLine along the path; carries the object; erases along the path
    Answer a controlnoTally bars; moves a racer
    SendnoFlies off, to a destination or into a collage

    Every action has a custom screen: the prompt, the layout and the controls are written for the scene, whether that is a press area, a drawing pad, a poll grid or a small game. What is shared sits underneath — the same few messages (Section 5.1), the same cursor, the same set of dome results. Where a scene runs its own code on the phone, such as a game loop, the phone still reports results (a score, progress, a finish) rather than frames.

    Answering and sending need no cursor, so they need no calibration and latecomers can take part in them straight away.

    Two details matter. A press is reported twice, as down and up, because a line and a drag both need to know when the press ended. And during dome interactions the phone shows big, bold buttons (Section 1.3): one large Press button, plus a second only when a scene needs it, such as Send. The phone never shows a tool menu; the scene decides what a press means.

    Order is fixed and short: get, then act, then release.

    3.4Readers

    Two things make sound. An action makes its own, heard by the person who made it: a tap clicks, a drop lands, a send leaves. A reader makes the other: it turns what the room is doing into a part everybody hears. There are two kinds.

    • Path readers move a playhead over the dome and sound whatever it passes, whoever put it there and whenever. The AV looper studies are path readers. They need content that sits still: stamps, placed shapes, drawings, words.
    • The action reader has no playhead. The stream of incoming actions is the part: every vote, tap or press sounds as it arrives, from its sender's seat, and the rhythm is the room's. It suits scenes where nothing accumulates on the dome — a poll, a race, a spatial vote — and works before anyone has placed anything.
    Table 4: What a reader is.
    SettingOptions
    PathA ring growing from the top of the dome outward; a ball falling from the centre under gravity; a hand sweeping around; or no path at all, the incoming actions themselves.
    SpeedLoop length and direction for a path reader; for the action reader, whether notes land as they arrive or snap to a grid.
    On contactSound that thing's voice, from where it sits on the dome — or, for the action reader, from the sender's seat.

    Two mappings are worth naming. Concentric: distance from the top of the dome is time and angle is output channel, so a ring of shapes plays as a bar. Ping pong: the playhead is a falling ball, contact is a bounce, and the rhythm comes from the physics rather than a clock.

    A path reader is a layer over dome content, not an action, so the same reader runs over a stamp trail, placed shapes, a drawing or a set of magnet words. Pointed at words it speaks them in the order it reaches them. Each thing on the dome carries a voice the scene gives it: a shape type to a timbre, a word to its recording, a drawing to a sample chosen by its size or length. Position is the pan: azimuth and elevation go to the spatial engine as they are, and the scene chooses whether a sound comes from where the thing is or from where its maker sits.

    Everything audible is also visible: the playhead is drawn, and a thing flashes as it sounds.

    3.5Try it

    The grid is every combination that works. Pick a dot, or a preset, and the chain below shows what was chosen; the phone and dome run it. Turn the sound on and put a reader over whatever is on the dome.

    4Facilitator

    A scene is one configuration of the system: what phones show, what the dome does with each action, and what ends it. A show is an ordered list of scenes. Both are written by the artist; the hub only executes them.

    Each scene gets its own phone screen, drawn for that moment. Underneath, every screen sends the same few messages and every dome result is drawn from the same set (Section 3), so a new screen is a design job rather than a new system.

    4.1What a scene defines

    Table 5: The parts of a scene.
    PartDefines
    PhoneThe screen: layout, controls and prompt, designed for this scene. Big buttons (Section 1.3).
    ObjectsWhere objects come from (Section 3.2), who owns them, and how many a person may leave.
    RulesWhich dome result each phone action produces (Table 3).
    DomeHow the result is shown: layout, what persists from earlier scenes, what is cleared.
    SoundThe voice each thing carries, which reader is running (Section 3.4) — a path reader where content accumulates, the action reader where it does not — and whether a note comes from the thing or from its sender’s seat.
    AdvanceWhat ends the scene: the artist, a timer, or a share of the audience finishing.

    4.2Building a scene

    1. Pick a chain from the grid in Figure 4: an object source, a phone action, a dome result.
    2. Set the limits: how many objects each person may leave, whether earlier scenes' objects stay on the dome, whether a person can act more than once.
    3. Design the screen: prompt, controls and layout. It can look like anything, as long as the controls map onto the actions in Table 3 and the buttons are big enough to hit without looking.
    4. Write the prompt. It is the only text most people read, and they read it while looking up.
    5. Decide what the dome shows when nobody is doing anything, and what it shows to a person who has not calibrated.

    Scenes are reusable: the same definition with a different word list, shape set or destination is a different scene in the show.

    4.3Sequencing a show

    A show is a list of scenes with a rule for moving on. Calibration is the first scene and can be re-shown at any point, which is also how latecomers join and how drift is corrected (Section 2.6).

    4.4Running it

    • Console. The artist needs four controls: next, back, show the marker, and blackout. The console is another client of the hub, so it can be a laptop, a phone or a cue sent from the visuals software.
    • Quorum. Advancing on a share of the audience rather than all of it, since some phones will always be idle.
    • Joining mid-show. A phone that joins during a scene gets the current prompt; if the scene needs a cursor and the phone is not calibrated, it is offered the marker instead.
    • Falling back. Any scene should still read as an image if nobody participates.

    5Hub

    The hub keeps track of every phone, runs the scene the facilitator has chosen (Section 4), drives the output and logs everything.

    5.1What phones send

    Table 6: Phone to hub messages.
    MessageContentsSection
    hellophone id, screen size—
    calibmarker dot pixels and sensor angles; then angles at the front-mark check2.3
    aimsensor angles, 30 per second while aiming2.4
    pressdown or up3.3
    objectkind and content (or a file upload)3.2
    send—3.3
    inputcontrol and value3.3
    hidden / visiblephone left or returned to the app5.3

    Phones send raw sensor angles, the hub applies each phone's calibration. Aim is a stream: losing one doesn't matter because another is coming. The rest are events: each must arrive exactly once, so they are acknowledged and ignored if they arrive twice.

    5.2Output

    The hub sends OSC to the visuals and the spatial audio: cursor positions, objects appearing, moving, being placed or sent, poll results, scores. Positions are azimuth and elevation; the renderer applies the dome's size and projection. Dome content with no one holding it is output the same way, with no phone attached.

    Readers (Section 3.4) run on the hub clock, so the playhead that is drawn and the notes that are triggered come from the same tick and cannot drift apart. Each note carries the position of the thing that made it, which is what the spatial engine takes: azimuth and elevation, no conversion.

    5.3Logging

    Every message is logged with the hub's time, the phone id, the scene, and the phone's seat position from calibration. Aim messages are thinned to five per second.

    • Joining: how many phones connect, allow permissions, finish calibration, and how long it takes.
    • Accuracy: calibration results, and how far presses land from targets.
    • Participation: who does what in each scene, and where in the room.
    • Attention: when phones leave the app (hidden).
